Points

Click points on a grid and get the simplest integer-coefficient curve through them.

April 2026

Live Demo Source

In 5th grade I played a game called Green Globs. Green dots were scattered on a coordinate plane and I had to type an equation whose graph passed through as many as possible. Points is the inverse. I click a few points on a grid and the computer finds a curve through all of them, as an exact implicit equation with small integer coefficients like x² + y² = 25 or x · y = 6. The solver looks for lines, conics, cubics, and quartics, in that order.

Null vectors of a Vandermonde matrix

An implicit curve of degree \(d\) is \(\sum c_j \, m_j(x, y) = 0\), where the \(m_j\) are the monomials \(x^a y^b\) with \(a + b \le d\). A conic has six of them: \(1, x, y, x^2, xy, y^2\). For \(n\) clicked points I build the Vandermonde matrix \(V\) whose row \(i\) is every monomial evaluated at point \(i\), and the coefficient vector has to satisfy \(V\mathbf{c} = \mathbf{0}\). Fewer points than monomials means the null space is non-trivial and an exact curve exists. Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting gives me a floating-point null vector.

To get simple equations I search sparse subsets first. For each degree from 1 to 4, and for each term count \(s\) from 2 upward, the solver tries every \(\binom{k}{s}\) subset of monomial columns and stops at the first term count that produces any exact curve. Among candidates with the same term count a score picks the smallest coefficients, penalizes curves that use only one variable, and gives a small bonus when both \(x^2\) and \(y^2\) appear, so a circle comes out as x² + y² = 25 rather than a lopsided equivalent.

The float null vector becomes integers through continued fractions. Each entry gets a rational approximation \(p_j / q_j\) with \(q_j \le 10{,}000\). I multiply through by the lcm of the denominators, divide by the gcd of the results, and reject the candidate if the lcm passes 100,000 or any coefficient does. Then the curve is checked against every point in i128 arithmetic, with no floats. If rounding produced a spurious null vector, the exact check catches it and the search moves to the next subset.

Where it goes wrong

The README promises \(y^2 = x^3 + 1\) for the points \((0,1), (0,-1), (-1,0), (2,3), (2,-3)\). The solver doesn't return that. Degree is the outer loop and sparsity the inner one, and nothing checks irreducibility, so at degree 2 it finds the four-term curve \(y^2 = x^2 + 2x + 1\), which is the pair of lines \(y = \pm(x + 1)\), and never looks at cubics. Both lines do pass through all five points. The unit test for this case only asserts that the output contains x^3 or y^2, so it passes on the wrong answer. Four points at \((\pm 4, 0)\) and \((0, \pm 2)\) return x * y = 0 for the same reason. What the program finds is the sparsest exact curve within a degree, and a product of two lines is a very sparse conic. I haven't decided whether to filter reducible results or leave them.

In the browser

The solver is 536 lines of Rust compiled to WebAssembly, called from a Web Worker with a 30 ms debounce. Clicked points snap to integers, though the solver scales half- and quarter-grid inputs up to integers if it gets them. The curve is drawn as the zero contour of the implicit function over an 800 by 800 grid of cells, checking each cell's corners for a sign change and interpolating the crossing along the edges. A native build solves the examples above in under a millisecond on my laptop. I haven't timed the wasm.
